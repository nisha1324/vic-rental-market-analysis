## The measure

A new lease is a rental bond lodged with the Residential Tenancies Bond Authority. The count measures new tenancies: newly built homes let for the first time, and existing rentals changing tenant. Volumes are 12-month sums (the four quarters to each date), compared for the 12 months to September 2019 and to September 2025. To tell new homes from turnover, new dwelling approvals from the ABS are summed per LGA from July 2018 to June 2024 (so most were built and could be let by the latest quarter) and divided by the LGA's private dwellings in the 2021 Census: the **building rate**. Approvals are not completions; some are never built.

**Not every growth area turned building into rentals.** Cardinia approved 19.7% of its 2021 dwellings, but new leases changed -12.7% and rents rose +53.4%; Casey approved 22.7% of its 2021 dwellings, but new leases changed +2.2% and rents rose +52.6%. The approvals data can't say whether those homes were bought by owner-occupiers, are still being built, or are let in ways the bond count misses.

**The City of Melbourne is the outlier the other way:** new leases +33.4% on a building rate of 15.2%. The 9 other well-sampled LGAs with a building rate within 3 points of it had a median change of -7.0% (range -11.5% to +33.9%); only Surf Coast (+33.9%) grew as fast. So the city's extra leases likely include more tenants moving in and out of existing apartments, not only new ones; the bond data can't say who they are.

## Why affordable leases rose where they did

Part 3 found a clear rise in the affordable share in a few LGAs. The change in the number of affordable new leases (share × leases, September quarters) splits exactly into a **volume effect** (more new leases at the average share) and a **share effect** (a higher share of the average number of leases). The share's own denominator is recovered as affordable leases ÷ share (only where the share is at least 5% at both ends, as the share is rounded to 0.1%). Across the well-sampled LGAs it is a median +4.7% above the rents workbook's lease count (which leaves out leases with no bedroom count), except Bass Coast at 1.8–2.0 times, a gap that has been there since 2000, so the two workbooks count that LGA's leases differently. The share effect means rents there rose more slowly than the affordability thresholds, which move with Centrelink payments (the 2019 thresholds aren't in these files, so that can't be split further).
